Forecasting Hitter Skills

Strikeout rate, walk rate, and power projections for 2027

Author

Andy Lucas

Project Overview

A hitter can take a step forward in several ways: make more contact, draw more walks, or do more damage when he connects. I built this project to forecast those pieces separately through strikeout rate (K%), walk rate (BB%), and isolated power (ISO). The central question is whether a hitter’s recent track record helps us project next season better than simply expecting a repeat of his latest year.

The final product is a searchable set of 2027 hitter projections, with model-based 80% prediction intervals for each skill. In R, I use reusable functions to prepare player histories, compare linear models with random forests, and evaluate forecasts across seasons. These projections provide a starting point for player evaluation; forecasting an overall offensive breakout would require a broader definition and additional testing.

Results at a Glance

The history models won validation for contact, walks, and power. In the 2026 evaluation, they reduced average absolute error relative to repeating the previous season by 10.0% for K%, 15.3% for BB%, and 18.2% for ISO. They also posted lower pooled historical MAE than the random forests for all three skills. That supports using a hitter’s longer track record, although the 2026 evaluation was exposed during development and is not an untouched test.

The first checkpoint is how well the selected models forecast 2026 from information available at the end of 2025. The table below compares each model with a simple baseball baseline: the hitter repeats his previous season’s rate. Lower MAE means the forecasts were closer to what hitters actually produced, on average.

Data and Forecasting Design

I use regular-season batting totals from 2010–2026, downloaded from the MLB Stats API through get_data.R and saved as data/batting_seasons.rds. K% and BB% measure strikeouts and walks per plate appearance. ISO measures extra bases per at-bat: (doubles + 2 × triples + 3 × home runs) / at-bats. It gives the project a measure of game power alongside contact and plate discipline. The report uses the saved data snapshot so rendering does not require another download.

To build a season-to-season comparison, I require at least 200 PA in the starting season and 100 PA the following year, with all three outcomes available. That keeps the focus on hitters with some major-league playing time in both seasons, although a 100-PA outcome is still a limited sample. Each eligible player-season pair counts equally in evaluation. Player IDs keep records aligned across seasons, and data checks flag duplicate records, missing seasons, implausible counts, and information crossing the forecasting cutoff.

Multi-Season Features

One season can give an incomplete picture of a hitter. I add the two preceding calendar years to give the model a longer track record. Three-year K% and BB% combine strikeout or walk counts and PA; three-year ISO combines extra bases and at-bats. A 600-PA season therefore carries more weight than a brief call-up, rather than treating both annual rates equally.

The history model also records how many seasons are available, whether the hitter played in the previous year, and how the target skill changed from that year. Earlier seasons with no record add no counts. When a previous-year comparison is unavailable, the change is set to zero and an indicator records the missing history, keeping eligible rookies in the sample. Everything comes from information available by the end of the starting season.

Training, Validation, and Evaluation Samples

I train the initial models on 2010–2022 starting seasons and compare candidates on 2023–2024 starting seasons, always predicting the following year. The 2026 evaluation then asks how the selected models performed using 2025 information. I had already examined preliminary K% and BB% results for 2026 during development, so I treat this as a development-exposed evaluation. The history features are judged against the same earlier validation seasons, and a future season will offer a cleaner prospective check.

Model Comparisons

Every model has to compete with the repeat-last-season baseline. A second benchmark carries forward the three-year PA-weighted K% or BB%, or the at-bat-weighted ISO, without fitting a regression. For hitters with less history, it uses the available seasons. This asks whether the history model adds value beyond simply smoothing a hitter’s recent results. Both benchmarks remain comparisons; model selection is among the fitted candidates. Rate-only regression asks how much of a hitter’s latest rate tends to carry into the next year. Expanded regression adds current K%, BB%, ISO, age, and log(PA), allowing the broader offensive profile to inform each forecast. History regression adds the longer track record. Random forests test whether more flexible relationships improve predictions, using current rates, age, and PA with 500 trees, two predictors considered per split, and nodesize = 20.

I select a model for each skill using the lowest pooled validation MAE, then check RMSE, bias, and the individual seasons for context. Contact, discipline, and power do not have to favor the same model. Random forests use the same player-season samples as the regressions, so they earn their place through forecast performance. The current interval calculations support linear models; if a forest wins, the report stops so its uncertainty can be handled appropriately before producing projections.

MAE answers a practical question: how far off was the typical forecast, on average? RMSE gives more weight to the biggest misses. Bias shows whether the model leaned high or low, using predicted minus actual. K% and BB% errors are reported in percentage points (pp): a forecast of 20% against an actual 23% misses by 3 pp. ISO errors remain in ISO units. All candidates use the same scoring function.

Rolling Historical Backtest

Before putting much weight on the latest evaluation, I check how the models would have handled earlier seasons. For each forecast year from 2016–2025, I refit every candidate using only outcomes available through the preceding year, then predict from the hitter’s prior-season information. The training window grows as more seasons become available, using the same functions for each skill.

This backtest asks whether the value of a longer track record holds up across different baseball seasons. The candidate settings stay fixed throughout. These are retrospective checks that overlap the validation years, so they provide evidence about stability rather than additional untouched tests. The shortened 2020 season also changes which hitters clear the PA thresholds and deserves consideration when reading the year-to-year results.

What Does the Model Add Beyond a Longer Track Record?

The three-year benchmark gives the model a tougher comparison than repeating one season. The table below scores both benchmarks and the validation-selected model on exactly the same 2026 hitters. A lower model MAE than the three-year benchmark indicates that the fitted relationships added value beyond using a longer sample alone. This comparison does not change model selection or the 2027 projections. In this evaluation, the history models beat the three-year benchmark for all three skills. The K% and BB% advantages are small, while the ISO difference is larger. Much of the improvement over repeating last season therefore comes from using a longer track record, with the regression adding a further edge whose size depends on the skill. These differences have not been established as statistically significant.

Power Forecasts

Above the diagonal are hitters who produced more extra-base power than the model expected; below it are hitters who fell short. The spread is a reminder that projecting game power remains difficult at the individual level. An improved average forecast can still miss a hitter’s big season or disappointing year by a wide margin. A projected ISO gain is a reason to look more closely at a player, rather than proof that his underlying power has changed.

How to Use These Forecasts

I would use these forecasts as the start of a player evaluation. Projected skill level and projected improvement answer different baseball questions: a hitter can remain a strong power threat without setting a new career high, while a hitter coming off a low ISO can project to improve partly because the model pulls extreme seasons toward more typical results. Sorting by the largest gain alone can overstate the case for a breakout.

Yordan Alvarez, Luis Arraez, and Ezequiel Tovar provide contrasting profiles for reading the projections. For each player, I would compare the recent sample, current rates, projected changes, and intervals, then investigate what pitch-level data or scouting observations might support that forecast. These examples illustrate how to use the table; they have not been validated as a breakout shortlist.

Alvarez’s sustained power production is essential context for this forecast. His projected 0.230 ISO and 13.3% walk rate describe a hitter who can do considerable damage while making pitchers work in the zone. The decline from his current 0.286 ISO is consistent with regression, rather than clear evidence of diminished power. His 80% ISO prediction interval of 0.172–0.287 leaves room for another season near his recent production. I would examine barrel rate, hard-hit rate, and swing decisions to assess whether his quality of contact supports another dominant season.

Luis Arraez: An exceptional ability to make contact

Arraez’s ability to put the bat on the ball is the defining feature of his offensive profile. His K% moves from 4.3% to a projected 6.1%, still describing remarkably few strikeouts. That increase is consistent with regression from an extreme contact rate, rather than clear evidence that his bat-to-ball ability is slipping. His 80% K% prediction interval of 1.7–10.6% leaves considerable uncertainty around the expected change. I would examine zone-contact rate, chase rate, and whiff rate by pitch type to determine whether any sustained changes support a stronger case for decline.

Ezequiel Tovar: A closer look at the power projection

Tovar’s projected improvement comes primarily through power, with relatively little change in contact or walk rate. Across 506 PA in 2026, he gave the model a substantial recent sample to evaluate. His ISO moves from 0.133 to 0.163, while K% changes by -0.1 percentage points and BB% by 0.3 points. More extra-base damage could strengthen his offensive contribution, but the forecast offers little evidence of a major change in his approach. His 80% ISO prediction interval of 0.105–0.220 also includes his current production. I would examine barrel rate, hard-hit rate, and launch-angle distribution to assess whether his batted-ball profile supports the projected power gain.

Limitations

The evaluation follows hitters who returned for at least 100 PA the next year. It leaves out players who did not return to MLB or received fewer opportunities, while the 200-PA starting threshold excludes many rookies and bench players. The results therefore describe rate forecasts for this eligible group, rather than predicting who will earn a roster spot or a full season of playing time.

Season totals capture what a hitter produced, but leave out much of the baseball context behind it. Contact quality, ballpark, opponent quality, injuries, and swing changes could help explain why a player took a step forward or fell back. A longer track record adds useful context without identifying the cause of development. K%, BB%, and ISO also cover only part of offensive value, so this project does not yet establish a probability of an overall breakout.

The shortened 2020 season and changes in the league environment complicate comparisons across years. Players appear in multiple seasons, and their outcome samples differ in size, which can challenge regression assumptions. The intervals assume common residual variance and may extend beyond physically possible values. I display them without clipping so those limitations remain visible; their observed group coverage is not a guarantee for a particular hitter.

I also keep the evaluation history in view: the backtest overlaps some validation years, and I examined preliminary K% and BB% results for 2026 during development. The final formulas are selected using earlier validation MAE, but the later evaluation still reflects that development process. Small model differences have not been established as statistically meaningful. Tracking these forecasts against a future season would be the next step toward judging how useful they are in practice.
